Cache Aside, and the Consistency Problems Nobody Warns You About

Putting Redis in front of MySQL is the easiest performance win in backend work. It is also the fastest way to create a new kind of bug: two copies of the truth that disagree.

This post walks through the Cache Aside pattern, the races it opens up, and which fixes actually work. Examples are in C++.

The whiteboard and the filing cabinet

Here is the picture I keep in my head.

Your database is a filing cabinet. Everything is in there. It is organised, it is correct, and it is slow. Walking over to it takes time.

Your cache is a whiteboard on the wall next to your desk. It holds a handful of things you looked up recently. Reading it takes a glance.

Two rules follow from this, and almost everything else in the post is a consequence of them.

The filing cabinet is the truth. The whiteboard is a convenience.

When the two disagree, you wipe the whiteboard. You never try to fix it up. You erase it and let the next person copy the answer down fresh.

For the code below, assume these interfaces:

1. Reading

Cache Aside means your application does the managing. The whiteboard does not know about the filing cabinet. The filing cabinet does not know about the whiteboard. Your code is the only thing that connects them.

To read something:

  1. Glance at the whiteboard.
  2. It is there? Take it. That is a hit.
  3. It is not there? That is a miss. Walk to the cabinet, get it, copy it onto the whiteboard, then use it.

People call this lazy loading. The name fits. Nothing gets written on the board until somebody asks for it. So every key misses once, right after startup. Usually that is fine.

One thing worth building in early. If the whiteboard falls off the wall, you should still be able to work. A cache outage should slow you down, not stop you. Wrap those cache calls so a failure logs and falls through to the database. Most Redis clients throw by default, and a throw here turns a cache problem into an outage.

2. Writing, and why you erase

To write something:

  1. Update the filing cabinet.
  2. Erase that entry from the whiteboard.

Two small decisions there. Both matter more than they look.

Why erase instead of rewrite?

The obvious alternative is to write the new value onto the board. That breaks the moment two people write at once.

A filed its change first but scribbled on the board last. Now the board is wrong, and it stays wrong. Nobody will notice until the entry expires.

Erasing does not have this problem. Two people erasing the same spot leaves the same result as one person erasing it. Order stops mattering. Whoever reads next walks to the cabinet and copies down whatever settled there.

There is a second reason. Some values are expensive to work out — a big join, an aggregate, a rendered page. If you rewrite the board on every change, you pay that cost even for entries nobody reads. If you erase, you pay it when somebody actually asks. And if nobody asks, you never pay at all.

Why the cabinet first?

Flip the order and the failure gets a lot worse.

A reader slipped into the gap. It found the board blank, went to the cabinet, and copied down the old value — because the new one was not filed yet. That stale entry now sits there until it expires. Could be an hour.

The other order has a race too. We will get to it. But it is a much smaller one.

So: file it, then wipe the copy.

One trap with transactions

If the write is inside a transaction, erase after the commit. Not inside it.

Erase before the commit and a reader can copy down a version of the world that does not include your change. You have just recreated the erase-first bug without meaning to.

This one sneaks in easily. The invalidation ends up in an ORM hook, or in a repository method that somebody calls halfway through a transaction, and nothing looks wrong.

3. The race that is left

Even in the right order, there is still a gap.

A reader misses, walks to the cabinet, picks up the old value — and then stalls. While it is standing there, B files a change and wipes the board. A comes back, does not know any of that happened, and writes its stale value onto a board that was just cleaned.

Cabinet says new. Board says old. Nothing will fix it on its own.

How often does this really happen? Less often than the diagrams suggest. It needs all three of these at once:

  • a miss on that exact key, and
  • the reader stalling between the cabinet and the board, and
  • that stall lasting longer than an entire write

Reads are usually much faster than writes. So the reader has to get descheduled at exactly the wrong moment, on a key that just missed, that somebody is writing right now. It does happen. At enough traffic everything happens. But plenty of systems look at those odds and decide to live with it.

Which brings us to the dullest fix in the post, and the best one.

Always set a TTL

Everything else here shrinks the window. A TTL puts a ceiling on it.

Think of it as wiping the whole board every so often, whether it needs it or not. It costs you a few extra trips to the cabinet. In exchange, every bug in this post has a time limit.

That covers the race above, a delete that failed, a key you forgot to invalidate, and the invalidation bug you have not found yet. Without a TTL, one dropped delete means wrong forever. With one, it means wrong for half an hour.

An entry with no expiry is a bet that your invalidation code is perfect. It is not.

4. Delayed double delete

This is the usual fix for the race above. Erase the entry, wait a moment, erase it again. The second wipe catches anything a slow reader scribbled down in between.

The wait has to be asynchronous. Blocking a request thread in sleep_for to serve a write is a good way to run out of threads.

Now, be honest about what this buys you. It makes a stale entry less likely. It does not make it impossible.

The delay needs to outlast the slowest reader in the gap. You cannot know that number. It moves with load, with GC pauses, with a bad network moment. 500ms is a number people copy from each other, not one anybody derived.

And it does nothing for the failure that actually shows up in production: the erase itself failing. If Redis is down, or the process dies right after the commit, both wipes are lost. Sending two requests to a service that is not answering does not help.

Worth knowing. Worth being able to explain. But it is a probability adjustment wearing the costume of a guarantee.

5. Binlog and MQ: the real fix

Here is the underlying problem with all of the above. The wipe lives in application code, in the same process that just did the write. So it can be lost the same way anything in that process can be lost.

Move it out. Let the filing cabinet announce its own changes.

Every cabinet already keeps a logbook. In MySQL it is the binlog, and it is how replication works. Tools like Canal or Debezium pretend to be a replica, read that logbook, and publish what they see onto a queue. Something on the other end reads the queue and wipes the matching entries.

Why this is better:

  • It survives a crash. The logbook entry is written as part of the commit. If your app dies a millisecond later, the wipe still happens.
  • Retries are free. Erasing is idempotent, so at-least-once delivery is exactly the guarantee you want. Do not ack until it works, and redelivery costs nothing.
  • Writers stop having to care. Every service that writes to that table gets correct invalidation. Including the ones you did not write. Including the DBA running a manual UPDATE at 2am.
  • Order is kept. Partition the topic by primary key and changes to one row arrive in the order they were committed.

It is not free. That is another system to run, and consumer lag becomes a thing you have to watch. The wipe is now eventually consistent — milliseconds to seconds, and longer if the consumer falls behind.

One requirement: binlog_format = ROW. Statement-based logging does not tell you which rows changed.

My honest advice. Start with cabinet-first, then wipe, plus a TTL. Move to the logbook approach when either the staleness starts causing real incidents, or when the number of services writing to your tables gets bigger than the number you can keep in your head.

6. The other write patterns

Cache Aside is the default. It is not the only option.

Write Through

Write to the board, and the board writes through to the cabinet for you, right then. One place to call, and the two never disagree.

Consistency is strong. But every write pays for both trips. And you fill the board with things that might never be read — the same objection from section 2.

Often paired with Read Through, where the cache handles the cabinet trip on a miss too, so your code only ever talks to the cache.

Write Back (Write Behind)

Scribble on the board, say “done”, and file it properly later.

Writes are very fast. Batching helps even more, since ten changes to one entry collapse into one trip. The catch is that you said “done” while the only copy was on a whiteboard. A crash loses it. And for a while the cabinet is not actually the truth, which complicates everything that reads it directly.

This is not exotic. Your OS page cache works this way, so do the store buffers in your CPU. But those have carefully built recovery paths. If you are doing it in application code over Redis, be sure you can afford to lose the window.

Write Around

Go straight to the cabinet. Do not touch the board at all.

Right for things written constantly and read almost never — audit logs, events, telemetry. Putting them on the board would push out things people actually read. This only works if the key is not on the board already. If it might be, you still need the wipe.

Pattern Write cost Consistency Good for
Cache Aside DB write + erase Eventual, small window Most things, read-heavy
Write Through DB + cache, in series Strong Read-right-after-write on hot keys
Write Back Cache only Weak, can lose data Extreme write throughput
Write Around DB only N/A Write-heavy, read-seldom

7. Three problems the diagrams leave out

Consistency is the famous cache problem. These three take down more systems.

Penetration — asking for something that does not exist

Someone asks for user:999999999. It is not on the board. So you walk to the cabinet. It is not there either. There is nothing to write down, so the board stays blank — and the next identical request walks to the cabinet again.

Point an attacker at that and your cache stops existing.

The fix is to write down the fact that it is missing.

Keep that TTL short, so a row created later shows up quickly. If the space of possible-but-missing keys is huge, put a Bloom filter in front instead.

Breakdown — one popular entry expires

An entry that thousands of requests depend on expires. All of them miss at the same instant. All of them walk to the cabinet. All of them ask the same question.

This is the thundering herd. It can take a database down in seconds.

The fix is to send one person. Everyone else waits for them to come back.

This collapses the herd within one process. With twenty instances you still get twenty queries instead of twenty thousand. Usually that is enough. If it is not, use a lock in Redis, or store an expiry time inside the value and have one request refresh it in the background while everyone else keeps serving the slightly old copy.

Avalanche — the whole board gets wiped at once

You warm the cache in a loop at deploy time. Every entry gets the same TTL. Thirty minutes later, every entry expires in the same second, and all of your traffic walks to the cabinet together.

Or Redis just falls over, and 100% of traffic hits the database at once.

For the first one, add jitter:

Ten percent of spread is enough to smear the expiries across minutes instead of piling them into one second. It costs nothing. Make it your default, not something you add after the incident.

The second one needs infrastructure. Run Redis replicated or clustered, and put a circuit breaker in front of the database so that when the cache disappears your app sheds load instead of forwarding all of it to something that is about to fall over too.

Wrapping up

The rules that survive contact with production:

  1. The cabinet is the truth, the board is a convenience. When in doubt, wipe the board.
  2. File first, then wipe. Erase, do not rewrite — erasing is idempotent and order stops mattering. Do it after the commit, never inside the transaction.
  3. Always set a TTL. It is the ceiling on every mistake you have not found yet.
  4. Jitter it. One line. Prevents a whole class of outage.
  5. Double delete narrows the window. The logbook closes it. Know the difference, and do not let 500ms stand in for a guarantee.
  6. Consistency is not the scariest failure. Penetration, herds and avalanches will hurt you first.

The whole thing comes down to one fact: a cache is a second copy of your data. Every problem above follows from that. You cannot make two copies agree perfectly without giving up the speed that made you want the second copy in the first place.

What you can do is decide how wrong they are allowed to get, and for how long.

Pick the weakest consistency your product can actually live with. Then make the failures cheap.
