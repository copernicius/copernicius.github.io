Some thoughts on business understanding

Tech stack is not business understanding

In storage and low-level networking, the words we say most often are “high throughput,” “low latency,” and “zero-copy.”

But try answering a different question. What real problem does the system you run solve for the business?

Most engineers I know answer with more tech. “I wrote a custom bypass stack on DPDK and got latency down to 3μs.” “I built a sharding and metadata layer on RocksDB and pushed throughput up 30%.”

Those are good answers to a different question. They describe technical experience. They are not business understanding, and the gap between the two is where a lot of careers quietly stall.

I used to think people in infrastructure got a pass on this. We work on the layer underneath everything, so surely the business stuff is someone else’s job. As long as my tech is solid, I’m fine. That turned out to be wrong. The architecture and the protocols exist to serve the business. If you don’t know what the business needs, you are guessing.

What “business” means when you work below the application

For an application developer, the business is orders, payments, risk control. For us it looks different. Our business is I/O patterns, workload shape, and hardware bottlenecks.

There is a version of this you can reach fairly early. You know which services call your RPC framework. You know roughly how many QPS your storage cluster takes on a normal day. When someone asks, you can add an endpoint, change a config, tune a parameter. That is competent, and it is worth having.

The more valuable version is knowing what actually hurts in each scenario.

In object storage, the pain is metadata lookups and garbage collection once you have billions of small files.

In block storage, it is holding P999 tail latency down and keeping failure domains isolated, so one bad disk doesn’t take out a blast radius you didn’t expect.

In high-performance RPC, it is cache false sharing, memory affinity on NUMA boxes, and how your congestion control behaves the moment packets start dropping.

Here is the part that matters for your career. If you switch companies, the specific RPC library you wrote might be thrown away on day one. The judgment about which hardware and which protocol fit which I/O pattern goes with you. That is the asset. You carry it in your head, not in the repo.

From running tickets to making the call

Plenty of engineers understand the mechanics. You know a write goes through the WAL, lands in the memtable, and eventually flushes to disk. Call that level one. It is necessary and it is not enough.

Level two is knowing the tradeoffs well enough to make a call.

Take DPDK. Level one is knowing how to use mbufs, set up rte_rings, and write a small user-space stack. Level two is knowing that kernel bypass is not a free win. Poll mode pins a core at 100% whether or not traffic is flowing. On bursty or uneven workloads you can burn a whole CPU doing nothing. You know when DPDK earns its cost, when io_uring is the saner choice, and when putting eBPF in front to drop junk packets early beats both.

Storage is the same. Random small writes love a WAL in front of an LSM-tree. That pattern is boring and battle-tested for a reason. Large sequential reads and writes are a different animal, and going through the page cache is often just overhead. Direct I/O, or NVMe-oF if you are crossing the network, tends to spend far less CPU per byte moved.

The day you can stand in a design review and say “here is how this pattern is usually handled, and here is the option that wins under our hardware and our workload,” you stop being the person who takes storage tickets. You become the person who decides how the thing gets built.

What if you are stuck on routine work right now

I can hear the objection. “I maintain one small storage module and I spend my days fixing bugs. Where am I supposed to find this grand industry view?”

Fair. There is no shortcut, but there is a path.

First, make your own patch of ground solid. Distributed systems and C++ punish surprises. A low crash rate, memory leaks you fix fast, and changes that don’t quietly regress performance are the price of admission. Nobody hands you a core component until they trust you with the small one.

Second, look past your own few thousand lines. Don’t only stare at the RPC handler or the storage engine you own. Watch how the services upstream call you. Could their retry logic turn a small hiccup into a stampede against your cluster? Follow the data across clients, gateways, and storage nodes and see where it really goes.

Third, build the whole map in your head. Once you have it, a new bottleneck stops being a mystery. You don’t reflexively open a flamegraph on your one module. You trace the map and say something like “this smells like cross-node NUMA access, or lock contention on the metadata path.” A sentence like that in a review lands right on the problem, and people notice.

The part that sets your ceiling

The further you get, the more everyone’s C++ starts to look the same. The locking tricks, the kernel knobs, the syntax. Everybody has read the same posts.

What separates people at that point is not how many C++20 features you can name. It is how fast you can find the real bottleneck in a system nobody fully understands, and how well you pick the fix.

The tech is the tool in your hand. The understanding of the actual scenario, the domain and where it hurts, is the thing you stand on.
